Cold-Weather Battery Guide

Sleeper-cab Li-ion that survives −60 F and 125 F sun

Jump to Section
Overview & Cold-End Failure Stack
The premise: a sleeper-cab battery that has to work at both −60 F on winter mornings and inside a 125 F sun-soaked cab in summer. The cold end is a materials problem. The hot end is a shade & emissivity problem — not a chemistry one.

Why conventional Li-ion cells die below −50 C

Ranked by dominance:

#MechanismApprox. loss, 25 → −51 C
1Ionic transport in the electrolyte
σ ∝ exp(−Ea/kT), Ea ≈ 0.35 eV		~100× conductivity drop
2SEI / interfacial charge-transfer resistance (Rct)~1000× — this is what actually kills performance. Interfacial kinetics freeze long before the bulk electrolyte solidifies.
3Kinetic Li plating on chargeNot thermodynamic — happens because ion transport can't keep up with the demanded current. Once plated, dendrites risk shorting.
4Bulk electrolyte viscosity / freeze-outThe final straw — the electrolyte literally turns solid or glassy.
Practical implication: a cell that reads OK at open-circuit voltage in the cold can still fail catastrophically the moment you demand current. Charge at low temperature is the danger zone — always limit charge current to C/20 or less below −30 C to avoid plating.

Hot-end note (125 F sleeper cab)

This is not a materials problem. Solve it with geometry: radiative barriers, reflective shade over the battery box, high-emissivity coatings on the surface radiating to sky. A thermal envelope for a sleeper-cab battery in full sun is a heat-rejection design task, not an electrochemical one.

Chemistry Options in Detail

Option A: Liquefied-gas electrolytes (usually skip)

Fluoromethane-based systems (e.g. Meng/UCSD) run as liquids under modest pressure and retain ionic mobility down to −80 C. Published, real. But:

  • Fluoromethane is a potent greenhouse gas (GWP ≈ 1500 if released).
  • Synthesizing it off-grid is energy-intensive and chemically hostile.
  • Cells must be sealed pressure vessels.

Cross off the outdoor, low-infrastructure list. Sealed cells contain the gas safely; making the gas is a different problem.

Option B: LATP solid-state (freeze-proof)

Target phase: Li₁₃Al₀₃Ti₁₇(PO₄)₃ — a NASICON-type oxide ceramic.

  • Sinters at 900–1000 C.
  • No expensive critical elements: skip LLZO (La cost wall), skip LAGP (Ge cost), skip sulfide argyrodites (hydrolyze to H₂S on any moisture — disqualifying in a sleeper cab).
  • Every precursor is scrap-sourceable — see the LATP section.
  • Once sintered, no freeze is possible — there is no liquid in the cell.

Option C: Mars-rover ester electrolyte (fast path)

The Spirit and Opportunity rovers carried rechargeable Li-ion cells that survived diurnal swings +20 C → −100 C at night. The electrolyte was not exotic — it was a carbonate + ester blend using ordinary LiPF₆ salt:

1.0 M LiPF₆ in EC : EMC : methyl butyrate (or ethyl butyrate, or methyl propionate), roughly 1:1:1 by volume

These systems hold ionic conductivity above 1 mS/cm at −60 C and support charge/discharge without lithium plating at moderate rates. Direct ancestor of today's "ester-cosolvent LiFSI" papers, but LiPF₆ works and is vastly easier to get.

Ester, not ether. The starting fluid you squirt into a cold tractor intake is diethyl ether. Ethers oxidize at the cathode above ~4.0 V in Li-ion cells — not ideal unless running low-voltage chemistries. Esters (methyl acetate, ethyl acetate, methyl propionate) have no oxygen–oxygen bond, are more oxidatively stable, and work up to 4.3 V with LFP or NMC. The idea is directionally smart, but the chemistry family is different.

Ester Electrolyte (Option C) — Recipe & Drying

Target recipe

1.0 M LiPF₆ in EC : DMC : EA (or MA) = 1 : 1 : 2 by volume
  • EC (ethylene carbonate) — already in the 1 M LiPF₆ base electrolyte you buy premixed. Solid at room temperature.
  • DMC (dimethyl carbonate) — also in the premix.
  • EA (ethyl acetate) or MA — added by you after drying.
  • VC (vinylene carbonate) additive — 2–3 wt%. Sacrificial SEI-former. Without it, first-cycle Coulombic efficiency can be as low as 50%; with it, >80%. Highly recommended.

Expected liquidus below −70 C. Conductivity ~2–3 mS/cm at −40 C, degrading gracefully toward −60 C. Works with graphite anodes and LFP cathodes — no special electrode modifications required.

LiPF₆ hazard: releases toxic HF on contact with moisture or skin oils. Work outside or in a fume hood. Nitrile gloves, eye protection. Have a tub of saturated baking-soda solution nearby to neutralize spills. Relative humidity below 5% is ideal — see the Dry Box section.

Drying hardware-store ethyl acetate to <20 ppm water

Hardware-store EA (often labelled "MEK substitute") is typically ≥95% ethyl acetate with the balance ethanol or water. Both need drying.

Materials

  • 3Å molecular sieves (beads or pellets). 3Å traps water and excludes ethanol; 4Å traps both. For EA, 3Å is preferred. Get 500 g per litre of solvent.
  • Glass jar with a metal screw lid (or clean HDPE, but test the lid liner — EA attacks some plastics). Aluminium foil for the lid seal.
  • Toaster oven or similar reaching 250–300 C for sieve activation.

Sieve activation (must be done first)

1
Spread sieves in a metal tray
Bake at 250–300 C for at least 4 hours, preferably overnight.
2
Transfer to a mason jar while still hot
Seal immediately and let cool. This is your activated sieve stock. Opened sieves pick up moisture from ambient air in minutes.

Drying the EA

1
Filter first if particulate
Coffee filter is fine.
2
Add activated 3Å sieves
Roughly 20% by weight (200 g sieves per litre of EA).
3
Seal tightly, sit at least 1 week
Aluminium foil under the lid liner if needed. Agitate daily. Cool, dark place.
4
Second-stage sieve treatment
To get below 20 ppm, decant the liquid (leaving the sieve layer behind) into a fresh jar with newly-activated sieves. Let sit a few more days.
Cheat: buy "anhydrous" ethyl acetate (>99.5%, <100 ppm water) from a lab supply. Sieves then drop that from ~100 ppm to sub-20 easily. Recommended for a first build. Hardware-store EA works with patience and double-drying.

How dry is dry enough?

  • Cobalt chloride silica gel strip inside the jar: blue = dry, pink = wet. Not quantitative below ~10% RH but a clear danger signal.
  • Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate powder in a small dish: pure white = dry; blue = moisture. Very sensitive.
  • Cell test: if the first-cycle Coulombic efficiency of a fresh graphite half-cell is >85%, water is low enough. If you see gross gassing and puffy pouches, water is too high.

The salt: LiPF₆, not exotica

Mars rovers used LiPF₆ because it was the established industry standard. Every phone battery still uses it. Buy "battery-grade" 1 M LiPF₆ in EC/DMC premixed from a lab or battery supplier — then add your dried ester to tune the low-temperature properties.

Harvesting LiPF₆ from dead cells is possible but hazardous (HF gas if heated, requires true glove-box discipline). Buy it ready-made for initial tests; only consider cell cannibalization once you're fluent in dry handling.

LiFSI (lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide) is technically better but harder to source and often more expensive than the rest of the project combined. Start with LiPF₆.

Home-brewing an ester (optional side quest)

Fischer esterification: acetic acid (concentrated vinegar) + methanol (gas-line antifreeze "Heet" in a pinch) + acid catalyst → methyl acetate. The resulting MA must be rigorously dried and distilled. Fun chemistry, but purchasing a sealed bottle of anhydrous methyl acetate is cheaper, safer, and yields known-pure product. Home-brew only if there is truly no other access.

Cordierite is the load-bearing item

Cordierite honeycomb from dead catalytic converters is the single highest-value item in the whole process. It comes prefired. Thermal expansion coefficient 1–2 ppm/K. Doesn't crack from thermal shock cycling between −60 C and 900 C. Exactly what your kiln furniture, housings, and thermal barriers need.

Get as much cordierite as you can before you start anything else. It's your housing, your thermal barrier, and your kiln furniture before you sinter anything.

Why not the other solid-state families?

  • LLZO (Li₇La₃Zr₂O₁₂): lanthanum is the cost wall.
  • LAGP (Li₁₃Al₀₃Ge₁₇(PO₄)₃): germanium is the cost wall.
  • Sulfide argyrodites (Li₆PS₅Cl and family): highest conductivity (10⁻³ S/cm) but hydrolyze to H₂S on any moisture contact. Disqualifying in a sleeper cab where a fuel leak or a spilled coffee could kill.
The Kiln — Solar Fresnel + Microwave/SiC Backup

Nine Fresnel lenses from rear-projection TVs make a solar furnace. A large rear-projection lens easily hits >1500 C at its focal spot — past LATP's soak temperature. The problem is intermittency, not peak temperature.

Learned in blood (and singed denim): A concentrated solar spot on an unshielded surface will burn through jeans in seconds. A small child in the exclusion zone is a lethal accident waiting to happen. The BMS in bms/bms_1s_merged.ino includes a PIR-triggered safety shutter for exactly this reason. Do not skip it.

Design principle: don't sinter at the focal point

Sinter in a high-thermal-mass cavity. The focal spot heats a SiC susceptor plate; the plate conducts into a refractory cavity; the cavity holds soak through cloud gaps.

[fresnel array] → [focal spot on SiC susceptor plate]
                        ↓ conduction
                  [refractory cavity: cordierite honeycomb
                   packed in perlite/vermiculite, high mass]
                        ↓
                  [work zone holds soak through cloud gaps]

Thermal-mass sizing

Enough mass that the temperature decay rate during a cloud passage stays <5 C/min. Cordierite kiln furniture tolerates those cycles; alumina would crack. The SiC susceptor plate absorbs the concentrated light and transfers heat evenly into the cavity.

Backup: microwave + SiC susceptor

For nights, overcast days, or guaranteed cycles:

  • Consumer microwave (1000 W) with a SiC susceptor (small silicon carbide kiln shelf or rod) inside a perlite-packed alumina cavity.
  • Known technique, ~$40 in susceptor material, reaches 1100–1200 C — well above LATP's 900–1000 C sintering window.
  • Use the same cordierite/perlite cavity layout; replace the solar input with the microwave/SiC couple.

Fallback order

  1. Fresnel solar cavity (primary, zero operating cost).
  2. Microwave + SiC (night, overcast, guaranteed cycle).
Other uses for the same Fresnel array: tab welding (aluminium melts at 660 C, copper at 1084 C — both easily reached), sieve regeneration by baking in the focal spot, or slow solvent distillation with a copper coil through the hot block. The block heats a lot more than just the LATP.

Cell Assembly (Pouch)

Target: a small pouch cell (single or few hundred mAh) for testing. Graphite anode, LFP cathode — both harvestable from dead cells or bought as coated foils. Electrolyte: 1 M LiPF₆ in EC:DMC:EA (1:1:2 v/v) + 2–3% VC.

Do everything inside the dry box. LiPF₆ releases HF on contact with moisture. Nitrile gloves, eye protection, baking-soda spill neutralizer on hand.

2
Electrolyte mixing (inside dry box)
For 5 mL total: 2.5 mL of 1 M LiPF₆ in EC:DMC premix + 2.5 mL dry EA + 0.1–0.2 mL (a few drops) VC. Mix gently with a clean glass rod. Final salt concentration slightly under 1 M — fine for low-T work.
3
Stack assembly
Anode / separator / cathode / separator / anode (single cell, two anodes). Separator must extend beyond all electrode edges to prevent shorts. Bind lightly between glass plates with Kapton tape or a PTFE binder clip.
4
Tab welding and pouch sealing
Weld or crimp Al tab to cathode foil, Ni/Cu tab to anode. No spot welder? Fold the tab area over several times and use a small crimp connector or screw terminal. Tab exits through the pouch seal. 5

Syringe with fine needle, ~0.3 mL electrolyte per 100 mAh of expected capacity, injected directly onto the exposed stack.
6

7
Seal final edge
Under partial vacuum or slight positive inert pressure.
Expected performance: at −40 C, a well-built ester cell with LFP delivers roughly 40–50% of room-temperature capacity at C/5. At −60 C, roughly 20–30% — still usable. Charge at low temperature is the danger zone; always limit charge current to C/20 or less below −30 C to avoid Li plating.
Formation Cycling for Ester Cells

Ester co-solvents like ethyl acetate are notoriously aggressive toward graphite during the first charge — they can co-intercalate and exfoliate the graphite if the SEI is not built correctly. The formation protocol is everything.

The chemistry problem

In a standard carbonate electrolyte, EC is reduced early (~0.8 V vs Li/Li⁺) to form a protective SEI. Ethyl acetate has lower reductive stability and can slip into graphite layers before the SEI is solid, causing gas and structural damage. We counter with:

  • Slow, staged first charge.
  • VC as sacrificial SEI-former (2–3 wt%).
  • A hold at low voltage to pre-form a thin SEI before bulk intercalation.

Protocol (graphite / LFP pouch)

1
Wet rest
After electrolyte injection and sealing, rest at room temperature at least 6 hours, preferably 12, for full wetting.
2
Open-circuit check
Measure OCV. Should be 0.0–0.5 V for a fresh discharged cell. If negative or above 1 V — short or contamination. Abort.
3
Stage A — C/50 from OCV to 1.5 V
Allows VC and EC to reduce gently and form the initial SEI without ester interference. 6–10 hours.
4
Stage B — C/20 from 1.5 V to 3.0 V
Main SEI thickening and start of graphite intercalation. Low current prevents local hotspots or plating.
5
Stage C — C/10 from 3.0 V to 3.65 V
Top of charge for LFP. Once SEI is established, esters tolerate normal rates.
6
Stage D — CV hold at 3.65 V until current ≤ C/50
Full lithiation of graphite, relaxation of concentration gradients.
7
Rest 1 hour, then discharge at C/10 to 2.5 V
Healthy OCV settles around 3.35–3.40 V during rest.

Evaluation

CE = (discharge capacity mAh) / (charge capacity mAh)
  • Good: >80% with VC, >70% without VC.
  • Poor (<65%): excessive side reactions, likely gassing. The pouch may balloon. If ballooned but not burst, carefully pierce with a needle inside the dry box, let the gas escape, reseal with epoxy or a fresh heat-seal strip, and continue cycling.

Second cycle: charge at C/10 to 3.65 V CV until C/50, discharge at C/10 to 2.5 V. Second-cycle CE should be >98%. If not, the SEI is still forming — give it one more slow cycle.

Cold-temperature performance check

  1. Charge at room temperature at C/5 to 3.6 V + CV. Measure baseline capacity.
  2. Place cell in freezer or dry-ice bath (thermocouple on cell body). Soak ≥2 hours.
  3. Discharge only at the cold temperature, starting at C/5. If voltage sag exceeds 0.5 V at start, drop to C/10. Record capacity vs temperature.
  4. Never charge below −30 C at rates above C/20 without close plating monitoring. LFP is robust but graphite will plate if anode potential falls below 0 V.
Thermal Management — Solar Block Preheat

The Fresnel array heats a high-thermal-mass block (SiC or steel inside cordierite / perlite insulation). The block stores solar heat. When the battery is too cold to safely charge, a fan pulls warm air from the block through the battery box.

Hardware picture

  • Solar block: cordierite-enclosed SiC or steel mass, well insulated (perlite / cordierite honeycomb spacers). Thermocouple (Type K, or a pair of NTCs) buried in it.
  • Battery pack: ester cells in cordierite housing with an NTC on the cell body.
  • Thermal connection: insulated duct from block box to battery box. Damper vane inside the duct actuated by a small servo (salvaged RC servo, wax thermostat actuator, or dead-appliance solenoid). Small 12 V computer fan pushes air through the duct when the damper is open.
  • Ambient sensor: optional but useful for predictive logic.
  • Arduino: runs the BMS + thermal state machine, wired to the actuators.

Four operating states

StateConditionAction
STANDBYBattery temp > CHG_MIN OR block coldDamper closed, fan off. Normal BMS charging.
PREHEATBattery temp < CHG_MIN, block > BLOCK_MIN, sun available or block hotDamper open, fan on low. Move heat to battery.
CHARGE_WAITBattery temp rising but still < CHG_MIN, block heat depletedDamper closed, fan off. Wait for sun to reheat block or ambient to warm.
OVERHEAT_PROTECTBattery > 50 C or block > 300 CBlock damper closed, fan on full (vent to outside if equipped). Stop charging.

Predictive sunrise

A photoresistor (or a small solar panel voltage) plus a stability window predicts when the block will be hot enough for preheat. Simple time-constant model: if light > ~30% for 30 minutes, block will be > 80 C. The BMS could then pre-emptively start a tiny trickle self-warm (if some charge is available) to align battery readiness with block-heat arrival. That's the "BMS that reads the weather" the merged sketch implements.

Safety — What Can Kill You

Hydrogen fluoride (from LiPF₆)

LiPF₆ hydrolyzes on contact with moisture, skin oils, or ambient humidity to release HF gas and dissolved fluoride. HF is a bone-seeking cytotoxin — it doesn't just burn skin, it dissolves calcium in the tissue underneath, and severe exposure can cause fatal cardiac arrest hours after the initial contact.

  • Work in the dry box or a fume hood. Never bare-handed.
  • Have calcium gluconate gel on hand (2.5%) for skin exposure. Saturated baking-soda solution for spill neutralization on surfaces.
  • Ventilate any workspace where a pouch is being pierced or reseal is being done.

Concentrated sunlight from the Fresnel array

A rear-projection Fresnel lens can hit 1500+ C at its focal spot. It will burn through jeans in seconds, ignite dry grass at 10+ meters if aimed wrong, and cause instant retinal damage without proper filter goggles.

  • Enforce an exclusion zone with a physical fence, not a rope. Small humans are attracted to sparkly things.
  • The PIR shutter in the merged BMS sketch is the fallback when the fence fails. Test the shutter closure weekly.
  • Welding-grade IR/UV goggles when adjusting the array. Solar-eclipse glasses are not enough.
  • Never leave the array unattended pointed at anything. When you walk away, un-focus it or cover it.

Carbon monoxide (from any combustion)

If you're using the fuel-independence-guide's wood gasifier or a fuel-oil-fired kiln backup, CO builds up silently in enclosed spaces. Symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion. CO detector in every building with an engine or heater; ventilation on any exhaust path.

Methanol toxicity

Only relevant if you're home-brewing your own ester via Fischer esterification with methanol. Methanol metabolizes to formic acid → blindness → metabolic acidosis. Ethanol is the antidote (competes for the same enzyme). Skin absorption is real. Full description in ../fuel-independence-guide/index.html.

Ether (starting fluid) explosions

If you're tempted to use ether as an electrolyte co-solvent (don't — ethers oxidize at cathode > 4 V), the vapor is heavier than air, pools in low spots, and ignites from static or a hot surface. Never spray into a hot engine or turbo. Full note in ../fuel-independence-guide/index.html.

Lithium battery thermal runaway

Any Li-ion cell (ester or solid-state) can enter thermal runaway if punctured, overcharged, over-discharged, or heated above ~150 C. Runaway releases oxygen internally, ignites the electrolyte, and burns at 1000+ C. Water does not put it out — reacts with metallic lithium to release hydrogen.

  • Pack-level hardware fuse in the battery negative lead, in addition to the BMS.
  • Vent the battery enclosure to outside — never into the cab.
  • Fire extinguisher class D dry powder, sand, or let it burn out in a controlled area. Do not breathe the smoke (HF gas plus every other fluorinated combustion byproduct).
  • At −60 F, do not use lithium-ion for primary starting power — cannot charge below freezing without plating, and BMS heater death spirals are a documented failure mode. Lead-acid, kept warm, is the safe starting battery in extreme cold.

Caustic chemicals (lye, sulfuric acid)

Biodiesel production and Fischer esterification use NaOH (lye) and H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid). Both cause severe chemical burns. Goggles, rubber gloves, apron, water source. Always add acid to water, not water to acid.

Summary for the sleeper cab: a well-built ester cell in a cordierite housing with a coolant-loop preheater and a proper BMS is not more dangerous than any commercial Li-ion pack. It's more visible, which is safer — you can see the pouches, hear the buzzer, and know when something is going wrong. The danger comes from cutting corners on the dry box, the fuse, the vent path, or the exclusion zone around the Fresnel.
