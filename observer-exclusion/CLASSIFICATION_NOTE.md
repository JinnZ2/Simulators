The Hudson's Bay Company archive: continuous from 1670, post journals
recording daily occurrences of note, held at the Archives of Manitoba,
catalogued down to shelf marks — Albany post journals B.3/1 to 212
covering 1705 to 1941. And environmental historians are already mining it,
but for what it counts: population numbers, harvest series, climate.
Business History has a paper on knowing nature in those records. So the
quantitative extraction happened; the behavioural observation in the same
journals didn't.

Foxfire: twelve volumes plus a magazine running from 1966, and a physical
archive of collected interviews. Snake lore, hunting tales, planting by
the signs. Note the shelving — it's filed as folklore, in Appalachian
studies. The observation is in there and the category it was filed under
determines who reads it.

That's the mechanism, and it's cleaner than a missing channel. The
material was collected. It was classified as culture rather than as
observation, and classification is what removes it from the evidence
stream. A wolf behaviour account in Foxfire is a hunting tale; the same
account in a field notebook is data.

So this isn't observer exclusion by absence of channel. It's a third
thing: recorded, archived, and filed under a category that isn't evidence.
