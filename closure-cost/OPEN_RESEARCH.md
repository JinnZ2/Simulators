1. DATA COLLECTION — Breakdown‑Cones Recall Study

Gap: breakdown‑cones.json is the highest‑value case in the file and is currently unmeasured. Base rate is ordinary rather than remote, delivery is documented, and knowledge is checkable cold in a parking lot with no event required.

Knowledge state: NOT_STUDIED

Research question: Does procedure recall track self‑rated likelihood of personally breaking down, or does it track training record? Prediction: recall tracks the likelihood rating, not the training record.

Disciplines: Cognitive psychology, risk perception, safety engineering

Data sources:

· Drivers matched on years and training source
· Self‑rated likelihood of personally breaking down
· Procedure recall (free, prompted, recognition, demonstrated)
· Training records

Method:

1. Recruit drivers matched on years of experience and training source
2. Administer self‑rated likelihood of personally breaking down
3. Test procedure recall using all four recall methods (free, prompted, recognition, demonstrated)
4. Compare recall to likelihood rating and training record
5. Test the prediction: recall tracks likelihood rating, not training record

Expected deliverable: A data set showing the relationship between self‑rated likelihood and procedure recall, with the recall‑method effect quantified.

Falsifier: Recall tracks training record rather than likelihood rating (then the closure hypothesis is falsified for this case).

---

2. METHODOLOGICAL — Recall Method Standardisation

Gap: breakdown‑cones notes that free, prompted, recognition, and demonstrated recall give different numbers, and no cross‑case comparison is valid until the method is fixed.

Knowledge state: UNDEFINED

Research question: What is the correct recall method for measuring procedure knowledge in closure‑cost studies? How do the four methods compare in terms of validity, reliability, and sensitivity to closure?

Disciplines: Cognitive psychology, survey methodology, psychometrics

Data sources:

· Published literature on recall methods (free recall, cued recall, recognition)
· The breakdown‑cones study design
· Pilot data from the breakdown‑cones study

Method:

1. Conduct a literature review on recall method validity
2. Run a pilot study comparing all four methods on the same population
3. Compute correlations between methods
4. Identify which method best predicts actual procedure performance
5. Propose a standardised recall protocol

Expected deliverable: A standardised recall protocol for closure‑cost studies, with validity evidence.

Falsifier: All four methods produce identical rankings (then the method choice does not matter).

SCOPE_BOUNDARY.md

Why this framework is broader than standard non‑response analysis practice

---

The Problem

In non‑response analysis, things like prior closure, information availability, and diagnostic spend are not separate from the response. They are direct, material, contributing factors to whether a response occurs. When a post‑event survey says "people did not respond," that is treated as a finding about the event.

But non‑response may be a downstream readout of a closed prior — the event was rated as impossible, so procedure was never acquired. The response failure tracks the closure, not the event severity and not information availability. What gets called "shock" may be substantially the categorisation stall — the reading refused because it contradicts something held as permanent, with the delay spent recategorising rather than reacting.

---

Six Ways the Connection Gets Lost

1. The "Event as Cause" Fallacy

Many analyses treat the event as the cause of non‑response. If the event was severe, that is treated as the explanation for non‑response.

But a variable held at 2% and a variable closed as impossible produce the same behaviour right up until the event fires. After that they diverge — not in the estimate, but in whether a handling class exists. The event is not the cause; the prior closure is. If the analysis says "shock caused non‑response," it is not false for the event, but it may be false for the mechanism. The prior closure was causal — just not represented.

So "event as cause" often means "We treated the trigger as the explanation." That is a causal attribution error, not evidence that the event caused the non‑response.

2. The "Procedure Gap as Independent" Fallacy

Many analyses treat a procedure gap as an independent explanation for non‑response. If people did not know what to do, that is treated as the cause.

But missing procedure is not independent of closure. Protocol is not acquired for an event rated as impossible. A procedure gap can be a downstream readout of the closed prior rather than a competing explanation. If the analysis says "procedure gap caused non‑response," it is not false for the gap, but it may be false for the mechanism. The closure was causal — just not represented.

So "procedure gap as independent" often means "We treated the gap as the cause." That is a causal attribution error, not evidence that the gap is independent.

3. The "Information Availability as Sufficient" Fallacy

Many analyses treat information availability as sufficient for response. If the information was available, non‑response is treated as a failure of the individual.

But information availability does not guarantee response. Hawaii is the strong form of this. Pearl Harbor is the region's central documented fact. The closure survived it. Information was available; non‑response still occurred. If the analysis says "information was available, so non‑response is individual failure," it is not false for the availability, but it may be false for the explanation. The closure was causal — just not represented.

So "information availability as sufficient" often means "We assumed availability guarantees response." That is a psychological error, not evidence that availability is sufficient.

4. The "38 Minutes as Diagnostic Spend" Fallacy

Many analyses treat the Hawaii 38‑minute window as diagnostic spend. If people had 38 minutes and did not act, that is treated as evidence of failure.

But the 38 minutes is the duration of the error, not of anyone's decision. Diagnostic spend — time from receipt to first protective action — is not in the literature. Substituting the 38‑minute window for diagnostic spend would be proxy substitution. If the analysis says "38 minutes of non‑response," it is not false for the window, but it may be false for the mechanism. The diagnostic spend was causal — just not measured.

So "38 minutes as diagnostic spend" often means "We substituted the error duration for decision time." That is a measurement error, not evidence that diagnostic spend was the limiting factor.

5. The "False Alarm as Non‑Response" Fallacy

Many analyses treat deciding the alert was false as a form of non‑response. If people did not act, that is treated as failure.

But deciding the alert was false is a different response from failing to categorise it. Some fraction of non‑responders correctly inferred an error. The published work does not cleanly separate those two populations. If the analysis says "non‑response," it is not false for the behaviour, but it may be false for the explanation. The false‑alarm inference was causal — just not represented.

So "false alarm as non‑response" often means "We collapsed two different responses into one category." That is a categorisation error, not evidence that both are the same mechanism.

6. The "Retrospective Self‑Report as Measurement" Fallacy

Many analyses treat retrospective self‑report as measurement of prior state. If people say they did not know what to do, that is treated as evidence of a procedure gap.

But risk estimates are revised after the fact. variable_state = closed is inferred from the procedure‑knowledge finding rather than measured before the event. Retrospective self‑report is contaminated by post‑event revision. If the analysis says "procedure gap," it is not false for the self‑report, but it may be false for the prior state. The post‑event revision was causal — just not represented.

So "retrospective self‑report as measurement" often means "We treated post‑event recall as pre‑event state." That is a measurement error, not evidence that the procedure gap existed before the event.

---

What This Framework Does Differently

This framework treats non‑response as potentially a downstream readout of prior closure — the event was rated as impossible, so procedure was never acquired. The following components document mechanisms that standard non‑response analysis typically drops:

· Two branches of closure — Instrument branch: reliable intermediary becomes the reading; underlying quantity stops being sampled directly. Event branch: occurrence closed as not‑happening‑here; procedure never acquired or retained. They close different things and fail differently, so they are not merged.
· The prediction that separates them — Instrument failures cluster where the intermediary has a long correct record (inverting reliability scoring). Event failures cluster on remoteness of the rated probability, regardless of information availability.
· Information availability as the licensor of collapse — If information was absent, the procedure gap stands alone. If a documented local instance existed (Hawaii/Pearl Harbor), availability rules out the procedure‑gap explanation.
· Diagnostic spend as the operating‑side quantity — Resource consumed establishing what class of event this is, against resource available. Not estimated where the source lacks the numbers. Hawaii's 38 minutes is the duration of the error, not of anyone's decision.

---

The Knowledge‑State Vocabulary

When a variable or mechanism is physically relevant but not yet quantified, the framework records its epistemic state rather than excluding it:

State Meaning Example
not_separable The published record does not separate the mechanisms. The honest state. Hawaii: cannot separate closure from procedure gap.
UNKNOWN_ATM The mechanism is known to exist, but no current value is available. Diagnostic spend distribution in Hawaii.
NOT_STUDIED The mechanism is recognised, but no measurement has ever been attempted. Breakdown‑cones recall study.
UNDEFINED The variable has no agreed definition or measurement protocol. Recall method standardisation.

---

What Is NOT a Valid Epistemic State

RETROSPECTIVE_AS_PRIOR is not a valid knowledge state. If a variable is measured after the event, treating it as a measure of prior state is a measurement error, not an epistemic one. The prior closure does not care about our post‑event surveys.

The framework refuses to record a retrospective measure as a prior state. Instead, it records the variable as not_separable — the published record does not separate the mechanisms — and names what would be needed to move it to a quantified state.

---

The Standard

The question should not be:

"Did people respond?"

But rather:

"Was the event closed as impossible before it happened?"

If the answer is yes, the response failure tracks the closure, not the event severity and not information availability. End of story.

The response is already a function of prior closure. Our post‑event surveys, retrospective self‑reports, and collapsed categories are the only things pretending otherwise. And that pretense has produced a literature of non‑response that may be measuring closure rather than shock.

This framework does not pretend otherwise.
