# CASE — CORVID CACHING

*Domain:* comparative cognition / behavioural ecology

*The field's own name for it:* scatter-hoarding with cache-site memory;
observational spatial memory; cache protection.

## Species, kept apart

The informal version of this case runs three findings together under "crow".
They are three species and two genera:

    Nucifraga columbiana   Clark's nutcracker. Tens of thousands of seeds in
                           thousands of caches, recovered over months.
    Aphelocoma californica Western scrub-jay. What-where-when memory: which
                           cache, where, and how long ago.
    Corvus corax           Common raven. Re-caches conditioned on whether a
                           competitor could observe the original caching.

Only the raven is genus *Corvus*. No single animal is attested to do all
three, and the folder does not claim one does.

## What happens

The animal distributes items across a territory and stores their locations.
At recovery it goes to a site rather than searching for one. The raven
result adds a second stored variable: not only where the cache is, but who
had visual access when it was made.

## Readout

The cost lives in what you had to keep, not in the search. Cache the world,
and the problem is already solved when you arrive at it.

## What it does not pay

Recovering 5,000 caches over 10,000 candidate sites averages ~2.5 x 10^7
probes by search and ~5 x 10^3 by memory. The factor is not a saving. The
storage is carried continuously across the interval and is paid for caches
that are never recovered, by an animal that does not know in advance which
those are.

## What would break it

Recovery driven by local cues — odour, disturbance, conspecific attention —
with memory contributing little. The transfer then collapses into ordinary
foraging. For the raven result specifically: re-caching that tracks current
visual access rather than access at caching time.
