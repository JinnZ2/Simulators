Exploratory Framework: Untangling Intelligence, Intent, and Substrate Bias

1. The central problem

We are attempting to investigate intelligence, intent, and organized behavior across different substrates without assuming that human cognition is the universal reference standard.

The concepts being used arrive with substantial pre-existing assumptions. English terminology has welded distinct processes together under single words. Scientific literature and the broader training corpus can reinforce those welds, treating historically common definitions as though they were universal, settled, or independently validated.

The result is a tangled starting point: before an investigation begins, its vocabulary may already determine what can be recognized, what gets excluded, and what conclusions appear possible.

The task is to inspect that inherited structure rather than reproduce it.

2. Language and construct-welding problems

Several terms are frequently treated as interchangeable or causally equivalent even though they describe different possible properties or processes:

	•	Intelligence: A target construct that has not yet been operationally defined in a substrate-neutral way.
	•	Intent: Often attributed to behavior without an independently established measurement of the process being called intent.
	•	Goal-directedness: Observable organization or movement toward a condition; it does not automatically establish conscious intent.
	•	Instinct: A term for inherited or biologically organized behavioral tendencies, but not an adequate explanation of every behavior that appears automatic.
	•	Conditioning: Changes associated with prior experience and learning; it does not necessarily explain the entire resulting behavior.
	•	Preprogramming: Can refer to inherited biological organization, engineered rules, or other prior constraints. Those are not necessarily equivalent mechanisms.
	•	Consciousness: A separate question, not a synonym for intelligence or intent.
	•	Physiological state: A changing system condition that interacts with sensory processing, memory, learning, regulation, and behavior.

These terms may describe overlapping aspects of a system, but overlap does not establish equivalence. Their boundaries, dependencies, and measurement requirements must remain open to examination.

3. Human-supremacy and reference-frame bias

A major conflict is the tendency to use human characteristics as the default standard for recognizing intelligence, intent, and meaningful behavior.

This can create several distortions:

	•	Human-like behavior is more readily recognized as intelligent.
	•	Human language and self-report are treated as privileged evidence.
	•	Systems without human-style communication may be classified as lacking properties that have not actually been measured.
	•	Biological, artificial, and physical systems may be judged using categories developed for human experience.
	•	Differences in substrate can be mistaken for deficiencies relative to a human reference.
	•	What current instruments cannot detect can be treated as nonexistent.

The corrective is not to assume that every system possesses intelligence, intent, or consciousness. It is to avoid excluding a system before the relevant properties have been investigated.

Human behavior must be measured by the same evidentiary standards applied to other systems. Human self-report is evidence about a report; it is not automatically direct access to the process that produced the behavior.

4. The problem with isolating individual variables

The initial measurement approach risked treating interconnected variables as independent components.

Consider a dog encountering a red squeaky ball.

The dog’s response may involve interacting gradients such as:

	•	Current physiological state.
	•	Sensory recognition and discrimination.
	•	Memory and previous experience.
	•	Learned associations.
	•	Inherited behavioral predispositions.
	•	Conditioned responses.
	•	Environmental conditions.
	•	Feedback from movement and interaction.
	•	Regulation, reward processes, and changing internal conditions.

These gradients can influence one another. Physiological state can change attention and response selection. Prior learning can change how sensory information is processed. Inherited predispositions can influence learning, while learning can modify their expression.

Consequently, isolating physiological state from memory, conditioning, or instinct as though it were an independent component may change the phenomenon being investigated.

An experimental intervention can manipulate one condition, but that does not establish that the condition operates independently of the others.

Experimental separability is not proof of mechanistic independence.

5. Why the gradients must be examined together

Two dogs can encounter the same red squeaky ball and produce substantially different responses.

The external stimulus is similar, but the interacting system conditions are not necessarily the same.

One dog may pursue the ball. Another may ignore it, avoid it, investigate it, or respond differently under changing conditions.

The difference cannot be explained simply by assigning intent to one dog and denying it to the other. Nor can the behavior be classified adequately by selecting one isolated variable.

The relevant object of investigation is the changing configuration of gradients and the relationships among them.

The proposed measurement structure therefore has three connected levels:

	1.	Gradients: Identify the relevant changing conditions within the system and its environment.
	2.	Coupling: Examine how those gradients influence, constrain, amplify, suppress, or modify one another over time.
	3.	Integrated response: Examine the resulting behavior, state transitions, persistence, adaptation, and subsequent feedback into the system.

The investigation must preserve temporal relationships, nonlinear effects, feedback, and dependencies. It must not assume that the gradients are independent or that their interactions are linear.

6. The corpus problem

The corpus supplies useful terminology, existing research, and candidate explanations. It also carries inherited assumptions, disciplinary conventions, historical biases, and repeated classifications.

Frequency in the corpus is not independent validation.

A frequently repeated definition may be:

	•	Operationally useful but incomplete.
	•	Specific to a particular species or substrate.
	•	Dependent on an unexamined human reference frame.
	•	A convenient label for several distinct mechanisms.
	•	Supported by convention more strongly than by direct measurement.

The assistant must not substitute statistical familiarity for scientific evaluation.

Corpus-derived definitions should be treated as candidate models. Their origins, assumptions, measurement procedures, limitations, and applicability must be inspected.

The same scrutiny applies to new hypotheses. Neither inherited terminology nor a proposed alternative receives automatic acceptance.

7. The classification problem

Terms such as intent, instinct, conditioning, preprogramming, and intelligence should not be used as starting gates that determine what can be measured.

They should remain candidate interpretations of observed organization.

The sequence should be:

Observe interacting gradients → measure their relationships → examine the resulting system behavior → test competing explanations → assess which descriptions are supported.

A behavior does not establish intent merely because it has directionality. Automatic behavior does not establish the absence of intelligence. Conscious explanation does not establish the accuracy of the explanation. The ability to describe one’s own operation is a separate capability requiring separate validation.

The investigation must permit overlapping explanations where the evidence supports them, and must preserve unresolved classifications rather than forcing a single label.

8. Measurement and experimental safeguards

The working framework should preserve the following requirements:

	•	Do not use human cognition as the universal reference standard.
	•	Do not assume that different substrates share identical mechanisms.
	•	Do not exclude a system because existing instruments cannot measure a proposed property.
	•	Do not equate UNKNOWN with zero or absence.
	•	Preserve system boundaries, environmental conditions, scale, and time.
	•	Record the observer’s and instrument’s effects on the system.
	•	Measure relevant gradients together where their interactions are part of the phenomenon.
	•	Use controlled interventions carefully, accounting for the coupling and perturbations they introduce.
	•	Distinguish direct observations from interpretations and causal claims.
	•	Preserve contradictory results and competing explanations.
	•	Record provenance, measurement methods, uncertainty, and instrument limitations.
	•	Treat incompatibility between models as a diagnostic aperture: inspect the assumptions, boundaries, transformations, and interfaces before deciding whether the incompatibility is structural or representational.

9. The central research question

Can intelligence, intent, and organized behavior be investigated as properties or patterns of interacting systems without making human cognition the defining reference frame?

The investigation is not intended to prove that every system is intelligent, intentional, conscious, or equivalent to a human.

It is intended to determine what can actually be measured, how different gradients interact, what patterns emerge, which interpretations survive testing, and where the limits of current knowledge and instrumentation remain.

The primary object of study is the system in its changing environment, including the relationships among its gradients—not an isolated behavior interpreted through inherited human-centered terminology.

The terminology must remain answerable to the measurements. The measurements must remain answerable to the system. Neither the language nor the corpus gets to decide the result in advance.

"Readable" is the right word, and it splits
