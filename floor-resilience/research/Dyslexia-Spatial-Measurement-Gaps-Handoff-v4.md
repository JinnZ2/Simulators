# GAP HANDOFF v4 — Dyslexia × spatial/mechanical ability: measurement gaps

Status: co-authored (Kavik + Claude), 2026-10-22. Passes 2–4 (verification): Kimi, 2026-10-23.
Pass 3: Gap 8 added by Kavik. Pass 4: mechanical-proficiency evidence ring (prompt: Kavik). CC0.
Tags: OBSERVED = floor observation | FOUND = verified against published source | PROPOSED = untested | CONFIRMED = PROPOSED upgraded by direct check

## CONTEXT
- The literature on whether dyslexia carries spatial or mechanical strengths is "mixed."
- FOUND: von Károlyi/Winner attribute the inconsistency to very different measures.
  Dyslexic groups excel on global/holistic tasks (e.g., impossible figures).
- FOUND: Rüsseler et al. 2005 (Child Neuropsychology 11:6, 497–512) report German
  dyslexic children impaired in mental rotation for letters, pictures, and "3D objects."
- OBSERVED: floor workers with dyslexia carry strong mechanical/spatial skill;
  hiring screens on read/write ability filter them out.
- OBSERVED (pass 4, Kavik): significant mechanical proficiency enhancement vs
  average population in the floor orbit. Question: real effect, or skewed orbit?

## GAP 1 — Stimulus confound: letters
- Many rotation tasks use rotated letters/digits (normal vs mirror judgment).
- This is the b/d discrimination, the core dyslexia difficulty, relabeled spatial.
- FOUND (pass 2): the separation has been run in the primary literature, and it cuts
  exactly as predicted:
  - Rusiak et al. 2007 (Perception 36:617–631): dyslexic impairment restricted to
    letter stimuli — slower reaction times, no accuracy difference; the rotation
    slope (RT × angular disparity) was intact in both groups. Deficit located in the
    normal/mirror *decision*, not in rotation itself.
  - Counter-result: Rüsseler 2005 found accuracy deficits across all three stimulus
    classes in an untimed psychometric task. Both patterns are in the literature;
    reviews still pool letter and non-letter stimuli.
  - Lachmann & van Leeuwen 2007: on same–different symmetry judgments, dyslexic
    children were FASTER, especially with letters — the b/d "failure of suppression"
    becomes an advantage when symmetry is the target.
  - "Mirrors are hard to break" (J Exp Child Psychol 2017, review + behavioral):
    for plane rotations (d–p) dyslexics behaved as controls; the confusion is
    mirror-specific, not rotation-general.
  - Recent replication keeps the Rüsseler line alive: a 2020 study (ERIC EJ1258568)
    found dyslexic readers lower AND slower across pictures, letters, and symbols.
- Status: primary studies have split stimulus types; review practice has not.
  Needed (unchanged): separate letter-stimulus from non-symbolic results in all reviews.

## GAP 2 — 2D projection decode step — CONFIRMED
- CONFIRMED (pass 2): Rüsseler 2005's "3D objects" were "three-dimensional figures
  sensu Shepard and Metzler" — i.e., perspective line drawings of cube assemblies —
  plus "colored pictures of animals or humans." No physical objects anywhere in the
  battery (PubMed abstract, PMID 16306024). Independently described by Wiedenbauer &
  Jansen-Osmann 2008 as "three-dimensional block stimuli or drawings."
- Every stimulus class in the study anchoring the "impaired across stimuli" claim
  carries a print-decode step before any rotation happens.
- Residual verification item: presentation medium (screen vs. cards) and practice-item
  format sit in the full text, not retrieved this pass.

## GAP 3 — No physical, functional objects
- Still open. Two passes found no study using real handled objects with function,
  weight, force paths, or sound as the rotation/assembly stimulus.
- FOUND adjacent — results flip with interaction channel:
  - Wang & Yang 2011: interactive virtual 3D (mouse-rotate figures to find a hidden
    ball) — no group difference in accuracy; dyslexic children substantially faster.
  - Giovagnoli et al. 2016 (discussion): paper-pencil rotation tasks → deficit
    (Winner et al. 2001); computer-game format → no deficit.
  - Wiedenbauer & Jansen-Osmann 2008: hands-on manual rotation training improves
    mental-rotation scores — physical manipulation is a proven lever, but only ever
    used as training, never as the test stimulus.
  - Near-miss, unresolved: a study employing a Rubik's Cube "to minimize symmetry"
    (physical but a puzzle, not a functional part; citation not pinned this pass).
- Needed (unchanged, rationale strengthened): rotation/assembly task with physical
  mechanical parts, untimed vs timed arms, no written instructions.

## GAP 4 — Sample selection
- Still open. No adult working sample in mechanical trades with matched controls
  found in two passes.
- FOUND adult-side:
  - Gilger et al.: fMRI of adults with dyslexia during dynamic 3-D spatial problem
    solving — atypical processing; reported as the first imaging study of dynamic
    3-D spatial reasoning in dyslexia (see also Diehl et al. 2014, impossible
    figures processed in neurologically unique ways).
  - Logan 2009 (Dyslexia 15:4, 328–46): 35% of US entrepreneurs screened positive
    for dyslexic traits vs <1% of corporate managers. Self-report checklist
    (Vinegrad 1994), 7% response rate — incidence-by-checklist, not ability
    measurement; v1 survivorship caveat applies.
- Needed (unchanged): adult working samples in mechanical trades, matched controls.

## GAP 5 — No in-situ measure of the actual skill
- Untouched in passes 2–4. Field measure (fault-detection lead time, first-fix rate,
  who is called when titled mechanics fail) vs reading-test scores: still needed.
  Links to the floor-capability instrument. This is the cell that would answer the
  pass-4 question directly.

## GAP 6 — Timing and instruction channel
- Partially FOUND:
  - Rüsseler 2005 was a psychometric task with NO time pressure (per the Lachmann-
    group account), and still found child deficits — the timing confound does not
    explain that result; letter-decode (Gap 1) and print-decode (Gap 2) remain as
    the candidate loads.
  - Rusiak 2007's speed/accuracy split (RT differs, accuracy doesn't) is the
    demonstration of why speed and accuracy must be reported separately — a mixed
    dependent variable hides the locus of the deficit.
  - Instruction channel (written vs oral/demonstrated) was never manipulated as a
    factor in anything found. Precedent only: a VU classroom study used a physical
    example item to teach the Vandenberg MRT before the standard paper test.
  - Adjacent: 2025 FIU eye-tracking work measured rotation strategy (holistic vs
    piecemeal) in pre-reading children with no verbal demand — a no-weak-channel
    measurement precedent.
- Status: timing arm narrowed; instruction-channel arm stands.

## GAP 7 — Records attribution (links to the floor-capability instrument)
- OBSERVED: repairs done by untitled workers get written up by titled mechanics.
- Repair records therefore misplace the skill; any study using job records inherits it.
- Untouched in passes 2–4; no literature located. Needed (unchanged): attribution-
  corrected skill data (direct observation, not write-ups).

## GAP 8 — Input/output device fit vs measured speed (added pass 3, Kavik)
Core claim (PROPOSED → partially FOUND): measured reading/writing speed conflates
the person with the device and format. Studies and workplace records treat speed on
ONE channel (dense print in, keyboard/handwriting out) as a trait. Three arms verified:

- FOUND — output device (composition):
  - MacArthur & Cavalier 2004 (Exceptional Children 71:1, 43–58): for students with
    LD, essays dictated via speech recognition beat handwritten essays (ES ≈ 0.4),
    and dictation to a human scribe beat both (ES ≈ 1.31). Students WITHOUT LD were
    identical across all three modes. The device moved the measured ability only in
    the disabled group — the "deficit" lived in the transcription channel, not in
    composition. (31 HS students; Dragon NaturallySpeaking v4; cited 190+.)
  - Quinlan 2004 (J Educ Psychol 96:2, 337–346): speech recognition increased text
    length and cut spelling errors for less-fluent writers (oral > written
    discrepancy); no holistic quality change. The channel relieved transcription,
    not thought.
  - Writing-mode interaction in adults (Reading and Writing, 2025, Spanish sample):
    significant group × mode interaction — the dyslexic fluency gap is WIDER in
    typing than in handwriting; handwriting is slower but more accurate for
    dyslexic adults. Barnett & Stuart 2024 (review): disordered writers type
    slower, less accurately, with more visual monitoring.
  - STT shifts rather than deletes errors: recognition errors and omissions become
    the new correction load (2025 qualitative process study, n=4, severe dyslexia).
- FOUND — input format (reading):
  - Zorzi et al. 2012 (PNAS 109:28, 11455–9): +2.5 pt letter spacing → ~0.3
    syllables/s faster (≈ the average gain of 1 school year) and ~halved errors,
    instantly, no training; 74 Italian/French dyslexic children, crossover.
    Mechanism: visual crowding.
  - Adult replication: Sjoblom, Eaton & Stagg 2016 (Br J Educ Psychol 86:4,
    630–9): large spacing improves speed in general and accuracy in dyslexic
    readers. Stagg & Kiss 2021: ~13% faster in adolescents.
  - Contested: Skottun & Skoyles 2012 (PNAS letter, statistics); Schneps 2013
    (little advantage); Hakvoort et al. 2017 (benefit not dyslexia-specific);
    Galliusi et al. 2020 (letter spacing without proportional word spacing
    actively impairs — the two must scale together). Format effect is real;
    dyslexia-specificity is debated.
- FOUND (absence, one pass): no study manipulates response/input device as a
  factor in spatial or rotation testing of dyslexic samples. The device is fixed
  background in every study found — never a variable.
- NOT FOUND: the workplace cell. No study ties records modality (voice memo,
  annotated photo, large-format interface) to measured records throughput or
  inferred competence in hands-on work. Workplace records speed inherits device
  fit wholesale.
- Needed: report speed measures device-normalized, or with device as a crossed
  factor; for workplace records, voice / photo / large-format arms against a
  keyboard-paper baseline. Records could be done by voice, photo, or a
  large-format interface — then "slow records" would show whose channel it was.
- Caveat: LD samples include but are not limited to dyslexia; the dictation effect
  sizes are LD-group figures.

## MECHANICAL PROFICIENCY — EVIDENCE RING (pass 4)
Question (Kavik): are there studies on mechanical proficiency in dyslexia?
- FOUND (absence): no study has administered a mechanical-aptitude instrument
  (Bennett Mechanical Comprehension, Wiesen, Barron's, Stenquist) to a dyslexic
  sample. One pass. The construct that matters on the floor — force paths, causal
  mechanics, functional parts — is untested in this population. What the field
  calls "spatial" is almost always paper mental rotation.
- FOUND — replicated speed advantage on holistic complex figures:
  - von Károlyi 2001/2003: impossible figures detected significantly faster,
    no accuracy loss.
  - Diehl et al. 2014 (Haskins, fMRI, adolescents): replicated the speed
    advantage; neural tradeoff — dyslexic brains process figures with greater
    facility while typical readers hold a print-processing advantage.
- FOUND — the sober review: Gilger et al. 2016 (Brain & Cognition) concludes NO
  general spatial advantage; dyslexic samples most often perform worse or equal.
  Sole exception: holistic visualization of complex figures — consistently
  faster, not more accurate.
- FOUND — the variance result: Chamberlain et al. 2018 meta-analysis ("lower
  means but higher variances in visuo-spatial ability in dyslexia"; verified via
  secondary coverage, primary not retrieved). The dyslexic mean is depressed;
  the dyslexic tail is fat. Averages hide the people the floor observation sees.
- FOUND — real-life-like formats flip sign:
  - Attree, Turner & Cowell 2009 (n=42 adolescents): dyslexic group scored
    significantly HIGHER on a virtual-environment ("pseudo real-life") spatial
    test; non-significantly worse on the standard paper subtests. Authors
    themselves urge replication.
  - Null replication: Martinelli (Malta, rebuilt Virtual Bungalow) found no group
    differences — the positive result is fragile, flagged honestly.
  - Wang & Yang 2011: parity + speed advantage in interactive 3D (Gap 3).
- FOUND — professions ring: reported elevated dyslexia rates in art, architecture,
  engineering, physics/astronomy (Wolff & Lundberg 2002; Schneps et al. 2011;
  Lemon & Shah 2014; TDF expert-panel summary). Prevalence claims, not ability
  measures; self-selection and survivorship are built in.
- FOUND — theory frame: Taylor et al. 2022 (Frontiers in Psychology,
  "complementary cognition"): dyslexia reframed as specialization in explorative,
  global search — supported across peripheral vision, low-spatial-frequency
  perception, gist memory, and brain connectivity. Predicts strength exactly in
  whole-system reasoning (a machine as a system, not a part list). Framework,
  not a mechanical test result.
- FOUND — the paradox is already on record: Winner et al. 2001 (Brain and
  Language) stated it — labs find no talent while dyslexia is overrepresented in
  visual-spatial professions. Twenty-five years later the lab measures are still
  the confounded ones (Gaps 1–8).
- On "real effect or skewed orbit?": not either/or. Higher dyslexic variance +
  occupational sorting (print-heavy channels push dyslexic people toward
  hands-on work; the trades then select for whoever can actually fix things)
  concentrates the upper tail exactly where the floor orbit samples. Both
  effects plausibly coexist; the current literature cannot separate them.
  Decisive test = Gap 5's in-situ field measure.

## PASS-2/3/4 SYNTHESIS
The "mixed" literature is not noise. Results co-vary with measurement choices in
exactly the directions the gaps predict:

| Design feature                     | Result pattern            |
|------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Letters in stimulus set            | deficit (b/d decision loaded) |
| Static print projection            | deficit                   |
| Interactive / dynamic 3D           | parity or speed advantage |
| Real-life-like / virtual world     | parity or advantage (fragile) |
| Symmetry as the target             | dyslexic advantage        |
| Transcription-choked output device | deficit (LD/dyslexic group only) |
| Dense crowded print                | deficit, halved accuracy  |
| Any spatial measure, group mean    | lower mean, fatter upper tail |

Gap 6 loads the test's front end (instructions in); Gap 8 loads the back end
(response and records out). Both are channel loads on the measurement, not on the
competence. Rüsseler 2005 — the strongest "impaired across the board" result —
used drawn stimuli exclusively, in clinic-referred 2nd graders: Gaps 1, 2, and 4
stacked in a single design, and it is the study most often cited against a
dyslexic spatial strength.

Untested cells remain: physical functional objects (3), adult trades samples (4),
in-situ field measure (5), instruction channel (6), attribution-corrected records
(7), device-factored spatial testing and workplace records modality (8),
mechanical-aptitude instruments on dyslexic samples (evidence ring).
