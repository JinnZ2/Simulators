Lime: Burning, Slaking, & Using

Quick Reference

Burn Temperature
825-900 C (1500-1650 F)
Slaking Ratio
1 part quicklime : 3-4 parts water
Set Time (lime putty)
Days to weeks
Shelf Life (putty)
Indefinite under water

Types of Lime

TypeHow MadeUses
Quicklime (CaO)Limestone burned at 825-900 C. White, crumbly, very reactive.Slaking to make lime putty, whitewash, mortar. Keep dry.
Slaked Lime / Lime Putty (Ca(OH)2)Quicklime + water. Slakes violently, generates heat.Mortar, plaster, whitewash, limewash for buildings. Best aged 3+ months.
Hydrated Lime (bagged)Quicklime slaked with minimal water, then dried and powdered.Mortar additive, soil stabilization. Not as good for plaster as putty.
Hydraulic LimeContains clay/silica impurities. Sets under water.Foundations, exterior work, cold/damp climates. NHL 2, 3.5, 5 grades.

Burning Limestone

CaCO3 + heat (825 C) -> CaO + CO2
1
Build the kiln
Stack limestone and fuel (wood, coal, dry biomass) in layers. A simple clamp kiln or pit works. Ensure draft — chimney effect or side vents.
2
Fire to 825-900 C
Too low = incomplete burn (core remains limestone). Too high = overburned, dead lime (won't slake properly). Cherry-red to orange heat is right. Limestone glows dull red at 700 C, bright orange at 900 C.
3
Hold temperature 6-72 hours
Depends on stone size and kiln. Small stones (egg-sized) 6-12 hours. Large blocks 24-72 hours. Uniform size burns evenly.
4
Cool before opening
Let kiln cool completely. Opening hot lets in moisture and CO2, re-carbonating the quicklime. Result should be light, porous, white/grey chunks.
SAFETY: Quicklime is caustic. Wear gloves and eye protection. It reacts violently with water, generating steam and heat that can spatter. Always add quicklime TO water, not water to quicklime. Stand back during slaking.

Slaking

CaO + H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + heat (65 kJ/mol)
1
Use a metal or wooden tub
Not plastic — heat can melt it. Add water first (3-4 parts water to 1 part quicklime by volume).
2
Add quicklime gradually
It will boil, steam, and crackle. Stand clear. The mixture will reach 100 C easily. Stir with a wooden stick.
3
Let it settle
After reaction subsides, let it sit. The solids settle to a thick paste. Cover with a layer of water to prevent carbonation from air.
4
Age the putty
Minimum 2 weeks. Better 3+ months. Longer aging = finer particle size, better workability, stronger set. Keep submerged in water indefinitely.

Lime for House Coating (Whitewash / Limewash)

Lime putty + water to brushable consistency + optional binders

Basic recipe: 1 part lime putty : 1-2 parts water. Add salt (1/2 cup per gallon) or alum (1/4 cup) as binder to improve adhesion and water resistance. Add pigment (ochre, iron oxide, etc.) for color.

Application: Dampen wall first. Apply 2-3 thin coats, not one thick one. Each coat must be touch-dry before next. Works on stone, brick, earth, wood (with casein or linseed oil binder). Breathes — lets moisture escape.

Far North Tip: In cold climates, lime work needs temps above 5 C (40 F) to set. If applying in fall before freeze, ensure 3-5 days above freezing. Hydraulic lime (NHL 3.5 or 5) sets better in cold/damp than pure lime putty.

Testing Burn Quality

  • Good quicklime: Light, porous, white/grey. Falls apart when poked. Slakes vigorously.
  • Underburned: Dense, hard core. Doesn't slake fully. Re-burn or discard core.
  • Overburned / dead burned: Very hard, dense, glassy. Slakes slowly or not at all. Useless for mortar/plaster — use for road base or discard.

Sources of Limestone

Any calcium carbonate rock: limestone, chalk, marble, coral, shells (oyster, clam, egg). Crush to egg-size or smaller for even burning. Wash to remove dirt/organics — they cause uneven burning and smell.

Quick Reference

NH3
Boiling Point
-33 C (-28 F)

Concentration (household)
5-10% NH3 in water

Field Production from Organic Waste

Organic nitrogen (urea, proteins) + bacteria + time -> NH3 + CO2 + other compounds

Method 1: Urine Collection (Simplest)

1
Collect urine in a sealed container
Urine is ~95% water, 2% urea, and traces of salts. Urea breaks down to ammonia over time. A 20L bucket with a loose lid works. Keep it anaerobic (no air).
2
Let it age 2-4 weeks
Bacteria convert urea to ammonia. The smell will become very strong. In warm weather, 2 weeks. In cold, 4+ weeks. Stir occasionally.
3
Strain and dilute
Strain through cloth to remove solids. Dilute 1:10 with water for general cleaning. 1:5 for heavy degreasing. Full strength for stripping paint or heavy grease (with ventilation).

Method 2: Batch Fermentation (Higher Concentration)

1
Mix nitrogen source with carbon
Use manure, urine, blood meal, or fish waste. Mix with straw, sawdust, or leaves (2:1 nitrogen to carbon). Moisture should be like a wrung-out sponge.
2
Seal in a barrel or pit
Anaerobic fermentation produces ammonia gas. In a sealed barrel, the gas dissolves into any liquid present. A gas trap (water seal) prevents pressure buildup and captures some ammonia.
3
Collect liquid after 4-8 weeks
The liquid at the bottom will be ammonia-rich. Siphon it off. The remaining solids are compost.

Concentration by Distillation (Optional)

To make stronger ammonia solution, gently heat the aged urine in a closed container with a condenser tube leading to a cooled collection vessel. Ammonia gas boils off at -33 C but dissolves readily in water. The condenser can be a copper coil in cold water. Do this outdoors with ventilation.

Uses

UseDilutionNotes
General cleaning1:10 to 1:20Glass, floors, counters. Rinse after.
Degreasing1:5Engines, tools, machinery. Wear gloves.

SAFETY: Ammonia is caustic and the vapor is irritating. Never mix with bleach — produces toxic chloramine gas. Use in ventilated areas. Eye and skin protection recommended for concentrated solutions. In cold climates, ammonia solutions stay stable indefinitely but may freeze at high concentrations.
Storage: Store in sealed plastic or glass (not aluminum or copper — ammonia corrodes them). Label clearly. In sub-zero temps, dilute solutions freeze; keep in a heated space or use before winter.

Furnace Types

1. Charcoal / Wood Furnace (Simplest)

Steel bucket or pipe, refractory lining (fireclay, sand, ash), charcoal or hardwood fuel. Air blast from hair dryer or hand bellows through tuyere pipe. Reaches 700-800 C easily. Good for 1-5 kg melts.

2. Propane Burner (Cleanest)

Reil or venturi burner into a steel drum lined with ceramic blanket (Kaowool). Reaches 900+ C. Good for 5-20 kg. Propane consumption ~1 lb per kg of aluminum melted.

3. Waste Oil Burner (Free Fuel)

Drip waste oil (motor oil, transmission fluid, hydraulic oil) into a hot chamber with air blast. Very hot, very dirty. Requires good ventilation and a scrap steel chamber. Can melt 10+ kg.

Melting Procedure

1
Preheat furnace and crucible
Gradual heat prevents thermal shock. A cold crucible dropped into a hot furnace will crack.
2
Charge with dry scrap
Wet or oily metal causes steam explosions. Pre-dry in sun or near furnace. Small pieces melt faster than large. Fill crucible 2/3 full.
3
Flux and degas
Add flux (salt + cryolite 50:50, or just plain salt) to dissolve oxides. Skim dross (oxide skin) off the top. For degassing, plunge a dry piece of wood or a chlorine tablet (swimming pool) to release hydrogen bubbles.
4
Skim and check temperature
Metal should be fluid, slightly shiny. If it's thick and pasty, it's too cold. If it's smoking and burning, it's too hot. A piece of wood charred black in 3 seconds = about 700 C.
5
Pour into preheated mold
Mold must be warm and dry. Cold mold = cold shuts, misruns, porosity. Pour steadily, fill sprue quickly, don't stop and start. Dross stays in crucible if you pour carefully.

Making Molds

Green Sand (Simplest, Reusable)

Mix: 85% fine sand (screened, washed), 10% bentonite clay (cat litter — unscented, clay-based), 5% water. Mix thoroughly. Should clump when squeezed, break cleanly when pulled apart. Add parting powder (talc, cornstarch, fine ash) to pattern before ramming.

Ramming: Pack sand firmly around pattern in a flask (two-part box). Strike off level. Separate flask halves, remove pattern carefully. Cut sprue and riser channels. Reassemble, pour.

Lost Foam (Complex Shapes)

Carve shape from expanded polystyrene (Styrofoam). Embed in sand. Pour molten metal directly onto foam — it vaporizes and the metal fills the cavity. No parting lines, complex shapes possible. Vent gases well.

Investment / Plaster Mold (Precision)

Mix plaster of Paris + silica flour (fine sand) + water. Pour around wax or plastic pattern. Let set. Heat gently to burn out pattern (200 C for hours). Then pour metal. Good for jewelry, small precision parts.

SAFETY: Molten aluminum looks like water but is 700 C. Water contact = steam explosion. No wet tools, no damp floors, no sweaty hands. Wear leather boots, long pants, natural fiber shirt (synthetic melts into skin), face shield, leather gloves. Have a dry sand bucket ready for spills. Never use water on a metal fire.
Far North Tip: In extreme cold, molds and crucibles absorb moisture from air. Preheat everything near the furnace for 30+ minutes before use. Condensation on cold metal poured with hot aluminum causes violent steam. Store sand in a heated space or dry it before use.
Straight Rules & Straightedges

Principles

A straightedge is a reference, not a measuring tool. It establishes a line of perfect straightness against which other things are checked or cut. The three-plate method (scraping three plates against each other until they all mate perfectly) is the fundamental way to generate flatness from nothing.

Three-Plate Method (Generating Flatness)

1
Prepare three rough plates
Cast or machine three plates of similar size (6x6" to 12x12"). Aluminum, cast iron, or steel. Surface should be reasonably flat to start — within 0.010" or so.
2
Apply marking compound
Prussian blue, lipstick, or even charcoal + oil. Thin, even layer on plate A.
3
Rub plate B against A
Slide B over A in a figure-8 pattern. High spots pick up blue. Scrape the blue spots on B with a scraper (triangle scraper, hook scraper, or sharpened file edge).
4
Rub plate C against A
Same process. Scrape high spots on C. Now A and C are flatter, but B is still rough relative to them.
5
Rub B against C
Now B and C are compared. Scrape high spots. Repeat the cycle: A vs B, A vs C, B vs C. Each cycle makes all three flatter. 10-20 cycles gets you within 0.0001".

Scraping Tools

ToolMake FromUse
Triangle scraperOld file, ground to 3 edgesGeneral flat scraping, 60 degree cutting angle
Hook scraperOld file, bent and groundCurved surfaces, bearing scraping
Flat scraperOld file, ground flatPrecision flat work, fine finishing
Bull nose scraperRound file or rodConcave surfaces, radii
Grinding a scraper: Hold file in vice. Grind end flat (90 degrees to length). Then grind relief angle (10-15 degrees) on underside. Hone cutting edge on oilstone. Edge should be sharp but not razor-thin — it will chip. A burr is OK; it breaks off and leaves a fine finish.

Casting a Straight Rule from Aluminum

1
Make a wooden pattern
Oversize by 1/8" in length, 1/16" in thickness. Add draft (1-2 degree taper) for easy removal from sand. Make it slightly bowed (crowned) — scraping will flatten it.
2
Cast in green sand
Use a flat-back mold (pattern on a flat board, sand rammed on top). This gives one flat face automatically. The other face will need machining or scraping.
3
Machine or file to rough size
Leave 0.020-0.030" for scraping. If you have a mill or lathe, skim the faces. If not, file and sand flat.
4
Scrape to flatness
Use the three-plate method or rub against a known flat surface (granite countertop, thick glass, precision ground plate if you have one). Blue, rub, scrape, repeat. For a 12" rule, aim for 0.001" or better.
5
Mark graduations (optional)
Scribe with a sharp divider or etching tool. For a working rule, 1/8" or 1/16" divisions are practical. For a precision straightedge, leave it unmarked — it's a reference, not a ruler.

Testing Straightness

Without instruments: Place straightedge on a flat surface. Shine a light behind it. No light leaks = straight. Rotate 180 degrees and check again — this reveals twist. Check edge-on against a stretched string (gravity catenary is negligible over short distances).

With a feeler gauge: Place straightedge on surface. Slide feeler gauges under gaps. Record gap at each point. A 12" straightedge should have less than 0.001" gap anywhere.

Optical: Sight along the edge. Your eye is remarkably sensitive to deviations. Look for a straight line of reflection. Any wiggle = error.

Aluminum straightedges: Light, non-marring, easy to cast. But soft — edges dent. For a shop straightedge, cast iron or steel is better. For a field tool, aluminum is fine. Harden the edge by cold-working (peening with a hammer) or case-hardening with Kasenit if available.
Squares: Try Squares & Carpenter's Squares

Quick Reference

Target Accuracy
90.0 degrees +/- 0.05
Test Method
Scribe line, flip, compare

Generating a Right Angle from Scratch

Method 1: 3-4-5 Triangle

3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2 (9 + 16 = 25)

Mark 3 units on one edge, 4 units on the other, measure diagonal — should be exactly 5. Any multiple works: 6-8-10, 12-16-20, 30-40-50. The larger the triangle, the more accurate. A 30-40-50" layout on a floor is accurate to 0.1 degrees.

Method 2: Compass Construction (Thales' Theorem)

1
Draw a baseline
Scribe a straight line on your workpiece or a flat plate.
2
Mark center point
Pick a point on the line. This will be the corner of your square.
3
Draw a semicircle
Using a compass or divider, draw a semicircle centered on that point, crossing the baseline at two points.
4
Pick any point on the semicircle
Connect that point to the two baseline crossings. The angle at the semicircle point is exactly 90 degrees.

Building a Try Square

1
Cast or machine the blade and stock
Blade: 6-12" long, 1-1.5" wide, 3/16-1/4" thick. Stock (handle): 3-4" long, 2-2.5" tall, same thickness. Aluminum, brass, or cast iron.
2
Machine the mating faces flat and square
The face of the stock that meets the blade must be flat and exactly 90 degrees to the reference face. If you have a mill, this is straightforward. If not, file and scrape using the 3-4-5 method to check.
3
Attach blade to stock
Rivets, screws, or brazing. The joint must be rigid. If riveting, use brass or copper rivets (softer than steel, won't crack the aluminum). Peen the rivets carefully.
4
Test and adjust
Place square on a flat edge. Scribe a line along the blade. Flip the square over (mirror image). Scribe another line from the same starting point. The two lines should be exactly on top of each other. If they diverge, the square is off by half that angle. File or scrape the stock face to correct.
Flip Test: This is the most important test for any square. Scribe, flip, scribe again. Any deviation = error. A 0.010" gap at 12" = about 0.05 degrees error. For woodworking, 0.1 degrees is acceptable. For metalworking, 0.02 degrees is better.

Adjustable Square

If you want a square that can be set to any angle, make a bevel gauge style: two arms pinned at one end with a wing nut or thumb screw. Copy an angle from a workpiece, then transfer to a saw or machine. Simplest adjustable angle tool.

Levels: Spirit, Water, & Plumb

Spirit Level (Bubble Level)

How It Works

A curved glass tube (vial) filled with alcohol and a small air bubble. When level, the bubble sits at the center of the curve. The curve radius determines sensitivity: tighter curve = more sensitive. A 10-second level (bubble moves 1/10" per 10 feet) is standard. A 2-minute level is very sensitive.

Making a Spirit Level from Scratch

1
Make the body
Cast or machine a rectangular bar, 12-24" long, 1.5-2" tall, 3/4-1" thick. Aluminum or cast iron. One face must be flat (reference face). Scrape or machine it flat.
2
Mill or file a vial channel
A slot 3/8" wide, 1/4" deep, running lengthwise. The bottom of the slot must be parallel to the reference face within 0.001".
3
Make or source a vial
Commercial vials are cheap and available. To make one: seal a glass tube at both ends, fill with ethanol or methanol (rubbing alcohol works), leave a small bubble. The tube must have a slight curve. A straight tube won't work — the bubble won't center. Curvature can be ground into the tube or the tube can be bent slightly while hot.
4
Mount the vial
Set the vial in the channel with plaster, epoxy, or hot glue. Adjust it until the bubble centers when the body is on a known flat surface. Test by reversing the level end-for-end on the same surface — bubble should read the same both ways. If not, shim the vial until it does.
5
Add a cross vial (optional)
A second vial perpendicular to the first lets you level in two directions at once. Mount it the same way, testing against a known flat.
Testing a level: Place on a flat surface, note bubble position. Flip end-for-end. Bubble should be in the same position. If it moves, the vial is not parallel to the body. Shim or remount. Also test by placing on a flat surface and sliding a shim under one end until bubble centers. Measure shim thickness. Reverse level and repeat. Should match exactly.

Water Level (No Vial Needed)

The simplest, most accurate level. Uses the principle that water finds its own level.

1
Get a long clear tube
Garden hose, aquarium tubing, IV tubing. 10-50+ feet. Clear is best so you can see the water.
2
Fill with water
Fill almost completely, leaving a small bubble at the top. No air gaps in the line. Add food coloring to see the level easily.
3
Hold both ends up
The water level at both ends is exactly the same elevation, regardless of the path the hose takes. Mark the wall at the water level on both ends. Those marks are level with each other.
Accuracy: A water level is accurate to 1/8" over 100 feet if you hold the tube steady and read the meniscus (bottom of the curve) consistently. No moving parts, no calibration, works over any distance. In freezing weather, use windshield washer fluid or antifreeze instead of water.

Plumb Bob Level (Vertical)

See the Plumb Bobs section. A plumb bob establishes vertical. Two plumb bobs spaced apart, sighted along, establish a vertical plane. A plumb bob + a horizontal reference (water level or spirit level) = full 3D orientation.

Plumb Bobs

Principles

A plumb bob uses gravity to establish a vertical line. The center of mass must be directly below the suspension point. Accuracy depends on: symmetry of the bob, point sharpness, stillness of air, and length of line. A longer line = more accurate (swings less, settles faster). A 4-foot plumb is more accurate than a 12-inch one.

Quick Reference

Ideal Weight
8-16 oz (225-450g)
Ideal Length
4-6 feet
Material
Brass, steel, lead, cast iron
Point Angle
30-60 degrees

Casting a Plumb Bob from Aluminum

1
Make a pattern
Classic shape: teardrop or cone with a rounded top. 3-4" tall, 1-1.5" diameter at base. Include a loop or eye at the top for the string. Pattern should have 1-2 degree draft for easy removal from sand.
2
Cast in green sand
Two-part mold. The point should be in the cope (top half) so it fills cleanly. Use a central sprue. The eye/loop can be cast in or drilled/tapped later.
3
Machine or file to shape
Clean up casting flash. Turn or file the body to be concentric and smooth. The point is critical — it must be sharp and centered.
4
Center of mass check
Hang the bob from its eye on a pin. It should hang perfectly vertical. If it leans, the mass is off-center. File material from the heavy side until it hangs straight. Test by rotating 90 degrees — should still hang straight.
5
Sharpen the point
Grind or file to a sharp point, 30-60 degree cone. The point is the reference — it must be sharp and on the centerline. Harden if possible (case harden steel, or use a hardened steel tip pressed into an aluminum body).

Alternative: The Mason's Plumb (Simplest)

A steel nut, bolt, or piece of scrap with a string tied through a hole. Not elegant but functional. Weight should be 8+ oz for stability. A 1/2" bolt 4" long with a nut on the end works. File the end to a point. Tie string through a hole drilled in the head.

Using a Plumb Bob

1
Suspend from a fixed point
The suspension point must be stable. A nail, hook, or clamp. The line should be free to swing without touching anything.
2
Let it settle
A plumb bob swings in a decreasing arc. Wait until it stops completely. In wind, shield it or use a bucket of oil/water to dampen oscillations (plumb in a can of oil — the viscosity stops swinging fast).
3
Mark the point
The point of the bob indicates vertical. Mark the floor/wall at the point. For transferring a point up, use two plumb bobs at the same point and sight along them.
Far North Tip: In cold shops, a plumb bob line can ice up and stick. Use braided nylon line (doesn't absorb water like cotton). Keep the bob indoors until use. If the point ices, warm it in your hand or near a heat source before using.

Testing Vertical Accuracy

Two-point method: Hang plumb at point A, mark. Move to point B directly above/below A, hang again, mark. The two marks should be in a straight line. If not, the structure is out of plumb.

Mirror method: Hold a mirror against the wall with the plumb line visible in it. When the plumb line and its reflection align perfectly, the mirror is vertical — meaning the wall is vertical. Any gap = out of plumb.

Sextants: Principles & Construction

What It Measures

A sextant measures the angle between two objects — typically the sun (or star) and the horizon. From that angle and the time, you can calculate your latitude. With additional data, longitude too. It is the most precise handheld navigation instrument ever made.

Quick Reference

Principle
Double reflection
Arc Range
0-120 degrees
Precision
0.1 arc-minute (1/10 mile)
Mirror Angle
Half the measured angle

How It Works (Double Reflection)

Light from the sun hits the index mirror (on the moving arm), reflects to the horizon mirror (half-silvered), and then to your eye. The horizon passes straight through the clear half of the horizon mirror. You see both images superimposed. When the sun's reflection sits exactly on the horizon, the angle between the two mirrors is exactly half the sun's altitude above the horizon. The arc is calibrated to read the full angle directly.

Measured angle = 2 x (mirror angle) | Index mirror rotates with arm, horizon mirror is fixed at 45 degrees to frame

Building a Sextant from Scratch

Reality check: A precision sextant requires optical-grade mirrors, a precisely divided arc, and micrometer machining. A field-built sextant will be accurate to 1-5 arc-minutes (1-5 miles) at best — good for backup, not primary navigation. For real precision, scavenge mirrors from a commercial instrument or use front-surface mirrors.

Materials

PartMaterialNotes
FrameAluminum, brass, or woodMust be rigid. Wood is traditional (mahogany, ebony). Aluminum is lighter, more stable.

Construction Steps

1
Make the frame
Traditional shape: a pie-slice wedge, about 60 degrees, with a handle. The arc is the curved outer edge. The frame must be rigid — any flex ruins accuracy. Cast aluminum or machine from plate. Wood: laminate 3+ layers for stability.
2
Cut and graduate the arc
A 6" radius arc, 60 degrees of arc. Mark degrees with a divider: set to chord length for 1 degree = 2 x radius x sin(0.5 deg) = 2 x 6" x 0.00873 = 0.105". For 6" radius, each degree is ~0.105" along the arc. Use a sharp scribe and magnifier. Subdivide into 10-minute marks (0.0175" spacing). This is the hardest part — patience and good eyesight required.
3
Mount the horizon mirror
Fixed at 45 degrees to the frame plane, perpendicular to the arc. The mirror's edge should bisect the field of view. Half clear, half mirrored. Adjust so the horizon line is continuous across the mirror boundary.
4
Build the index arm
A pivoting arm that rides the arc. Pivot at the center of the arc's circle. The index mirror mounts on this arm, perpendicular to the frame, facing the horizon mirror. The arm should have a vernier scale for reading minutes of arc.
5
Add the telescope / sight tube
Mounts on the frame, aligned with the horizon mirror. Simple tube with a lens at each end (2x power). Focus on infinity. The tube should point at the horizon mirror so the eye sees both the horizon (through clear half) and the reflected object (through mirrored half).
6
Calibrate (index error)
Point at the horizon. Set arm to 0 degrees. The horizon in the mirror should align with the direct horizon. If not, adjust the horizon mirror or note the offset as "index error." Correct all readings by this amount. Test by measuring the sun's diameter (about 32 arc-minutes) — it should read 32'.

Vernier Scale (Reading Minutes)

A vernier lets you read fractions of a degree. On the arc, mark degrees. On the index arm, mark a short scale of 60 divisions that spans 59 divisions on the arc. When the 0 line on the arm aligns with a degree mark, you read the degree. When it's between marks, find which vernier line aligns with an arc line — that's the minutes.

Using the Sextant

1
Sight the horizon
Look through the telescope at the horizon. Keep the sextant vertical (use the swinging arc method: let the sextant swing slightly; the object stays on the horizon only when vertical).
2
Bring the object down
Move the index arm until the sun (or star) appears in the mirror, then bring it down to the horizon. Use the shade to reduce brightness. Fine-tune with the micrometer or by tapping the arm.
3
Note the time and reading
Record the angle (corrected for index error) and the exact time (to the second). Use a watch set to GMT/UTC. The time is as critical as the angle.
4
Reduce the sight
Use the nautical almanac (or an app) to find the sun's declination at that time. Apply corrections: dip (height of eye), refraction (air density), parallax, semidiameter. The result is your latitude.
Latitude = 90 - observed altitude + declination (if same hemisphere) or 90 - observed altitude - declination (if opposite)
Field simplification: If you only need latitude and have a noon sun sight, the math is simple: measure sun's highest altitude at local noon. Latitude = 90 - altitude + declination. No timepiece needed if you watch for the sun's highest point (it pauses briefly). This is how navigators worked for centuries.
Dividers, Calipers & Measuring Tools

Dividers (Transferring Measurements)

Dividers don't measure — they transfer. Set to a distance on a rule, then walk off that distance on a workpiece. Or set to a scribed line, then transfer to another surface. The most fundamental layout tool.

Building a Pair of Dividers

1
Forge or machine two legs
Steel rod or flat stock, 6-8" long, 1/4-3/8" thick. One end pointed (scriber), other end rounded for a pivot. Aluminum works for light duty but points wear fast.
2
Drill pivot hole
Exactly centered, same location on both legs. Hole should be a tight fit for a rivet or screw. The pivot must be tight enough to hold setting but loose enough to adjust.
3
Add a spring or friction joint
A leaf spring (clock spring, hacksaw blade) between the legs pushes them apart. A wing nut or thumb screw on one leg tightens against the other to lock the setting. Or use a simple rivet with friction — tap tighter to hold, looser to adjust.
4
Harden and sharpen the points
Heat to red, quench in oil or water. Temper to straw color (220 C) for toughness. Grind points to sharp cones, symmetrical. Uneven points = uneven spacing when walking.

Calipers (Inside & Outside)

Outside Calipers

Same as dividers but with curved legs that wrap around the outside of a cylinder. Set to the diameter, then transfer to a rule. The legs should be curved so the contact points are directly opposite each other (true diameter).

Inside Calipers

Legs bow outward to reach inside a bore. Set to the bore diameter, then transfer to an outside caliper or rule. The spring pushes legs outward; you squeeze to insert, release to contact walls.

Building Calipers from Sheet Metal

1
Cut legs from spring steel
Hacksaw blade, old saw blade, or leaf spring. Two identical pieces, 6-8" long, 3/4" wide. Shape the contact ends: outside calipers = curved inward; inside calipers = curved outward.
2
Bend the spring section
Leave the middle 2" straight for the pivot. Bend the rest to shape. The spring action comes from the material flexing. If steel is too stiff, thin it with a grinder.
3
Pivot and adjust mechanism
Rivet or bolt through the center. A simple nut and bolt with friction works. For finer adjustment, add a screw between the legs near the pivot — turning it spreads or closes the contact points.

Depth Gauge

A rule with a perpendicular base that slides along it. The base sits on the surface, the rule drops into the hole. Read the depth at the base edge. Can be made from a steel rule and a block of wood or metal with a slot. For precision, add a locking screw.

Transfer technique: The skill with dividers and calipers is the "feel" — the light drag when the points just touch. Too light = not contacting. Too heavy = springing the legs, distorting the measurement. Practice on a known diameter until you can repeat within 0.005".
Angles, Protractors & Bevel Gauges

Generating Angles from Scratch

Equilateral Triangle (60 degrees)

Draw a line. Set compass to the line length. Scribe arcs from both ends; intersection is the third point. All angles = 60 degrees. Bisect any angle = 30 degrees. Bisect again = 15 degrees.

Square (90 degrees)

See the Squares section. 3-4-5 triangle or Thales' theorem.

Pentagon (108 degrees)

Side = radius x 1.1756 | Draw circle, step off chord length with dividers 5 times

Hexagon (120 degrees)

Radius = side length. Step off radius around circle. Connect points. Every angle = 120 degrees. Bisect = 60 degrees.

Octagon (135 degrees)

Draw square. Mark center points of each side. Connect center points to corners. Trim corners. Each interior angle = 135 degrees.

Building a Protractor

1
Make a flat semicircle
Aluminum, brass, or steel. 4-6" diameter. Machine or file to a clean semicircle. The straight edge must be truly straight (diameter of the circle).
2
Find center and mark 0-180
The center is the midpoint of the straight edge. Scribe a line from center to the arc midpoint = 90 degrees. Use dividers to step off degree marks: circumference = pi x diameter. For 6" diameter, circumference = 18.85". Half = 9.42". Each degree = 9.42 / 180 = 0.0524". Mark every 5 or 10 degrees for readability.
3
Add a pivoting arm
A thin arm pivots at the center point. It should have a fine point at the center and a sighting edge or hairline at the outer end. A rivet or screw through the center, tight enough to hold position.
4
Calibrate against known angles
Use a 3-4-5 triangle to set 90 degrees. Use a hexagon to set 60 and 120. Mark corrections on the scale if needed.

Bevel Gauge (Adjustable Angle)

Two arms pinned at one end, with a wing nut or thumb screw to lock any angle. Copy an angle from a workpiece, then transfer to a saw or machine. Simplest adjustable angle tool.

1
Make two arms
Steel or aluminum flat stock, 6-10" long, 1" wide, 1/8" thick. One arm has a slot (3/8" wide, 4" long) for adjustment range.
2
Pivot and lock
Bolt through one end of both arms. Wing nut on the other arm tightens through the slot to lock any angle. A washer between the arms prevents binding.
3
Add a blade (optional)
A thin steel blade on one arm lets you slide the gauge into tight spaces. The blade should be removable or foldable.
