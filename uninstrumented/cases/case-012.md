# Case 012 — Fuel Cost Incidence on Substrate Goods, and the Aggregate That Cannot Show It

STATUS: open. Mechanism candidates: SCALAR DEMAND, PROXY SUBSTITUTION.
Not assigned.

READING PROTOCOL: markers to explore, not positions under defense.
Correct responses: test fit, extend, or report where it breaks.

---

## THE PUBLISHED FINDING THIS STARTS FROM

Diesel price pass-through in trucking is documented: roughly 50% of a
diesel price change reaches shippers immediately, near 100% within one
week. Carriers do not absorb it — thin margins, competitive market.

The same work concludes the consumer-price effect is limited, on the
grounds that transportation is a small share of total product cost.

That conclusion is where this case starts.

## Q1 — The averaging operation

"Transportation is a small share of product cost" is an average over
freight. Averaging over dollars places the weight where the dollars are:
dense, high-value goods.

Fuel cost does not scale with value. It scales with:

    mass
    volume / frontal area
    trailer class and its efficiency penalty
    auxiliary load (reefer runs a second diesel engine)
    permit / escort / route-restriction burden (oversize)
    tarping labour and materials (flatbed)

Value-density is the missing divisor. Transformer, generator, structural
steel, pallet of produce — heavy, bulky, low value per pound. Consumer
electronics — light, dense in value.

These are not one distribution with different tails. They are different
distributions, and the value-weighted average reports the electronics one.

EXCLUDED BY: the industry publishes rate-per-mile by trailer class
(flatbed roughly $0.70–$1.20/mile above dry van, 2026 spot data) and
publishes fuel surcharge mechanics separately. Neither is divided by
delivered goods value. Both halves exist; the quotient is not published.

WOULD MEASURE: fuel cost as a fraction of delivered goods value,
disaggregated by load class — dry van, reefer, flatbed, oversize/heavy
haul. Report the distribution, not a central tendency.

## Q2 — Substrate goods are inputs, not a category

The low-value-density classes are disproportionately capacity-forming
inputs: transformers, generators, pumps, structural steel, ag equipment,
pipe.

If fuel cost gates the delivery rate of the goods that build capacity,
the consequence appears downstream, months later, and is attributed to
whatever is proximate then. The link back to a fuel-cost step is not
recoverable from the aggregate.

WOULD MEASURE: lag-structure test between diesel price steps and
oversize/heavy-haul delivery volumes, then between those volumes and
downstream capacity commissioning. Requires Q1's disaggregation first.

## Q3 — Food, and the spoilage clock

Reefer freight is largely food. Two features distinguish it from Q2.

**Non-linearity.** A generator load postponed is postponed. A reefer load
past viability is destroyed. There is a hard discontinuity where the
economics cross. Smooth elasticity models do not generate
discontinuities — the functional form is wrong before any parameter is
estimated.

**Food is an input to labour.** Calories delivered to workers is a
precondition for all manufacture, construction and maintenance. It is
not priced as a supply-chain input to production anywhere in the
national accounts; it enters as household consumption, on the output
side.

The one input without which no other input can be produced is recorded
as a consumption category.

WOULD MEASURE: unclear, flagged as needing an instrument. Candidate:
delivered-calorie cost per worker-hour by region against local wage —
a proposed construction, not an existing series.

## Q4 — What the aggregate does instead

GDP real-output figures are quality-adjusted (hedonic adjustment). A
price increase accompanied by a claimed specification improvement is
booked partly as increased real output rather than as inflation. The
quality dimensions come largely from the producer's own account of what
improved.

Asymmetry: high-value-density consumer goods have claimable quality
dimensions. Substrate goods largely do not. A calorie has no new
features. A kilowatt-hour has no new features. A transformer's
specification is fixed by the grid it serves.

To test: the aggregate can be held level by hedonic credit accruing to
deniable-quality goods while substrate goods degrade in real terms, and
the two motions are not separable in the total.

WOULD MEASURE: decompose real-output growth into hedonically-adjusted
versus non-adjustable components; track separately over a fuel price
cycle. Divergence is the readout.

## Q5 — Lag and phase change

Shared structure: the aggregate registers after the substrate has
reconfigured. At that point the number is not measuring a slowdown, it is
measuring the aftermath of a transition it had no channel to see coming.

Same shape as Case 011 — the record holds the outcome, never the sequence
of states that produced it.

Whether a threshold exists past which the reconfiguration is not
reversible is stated as an open question. Not asserted.

## NOTE ON A CIRCULATING NUMBER

"$4.75/gal triggers measurable GDP drag" and "$5.25 correlates with
recession probability above 40%" circulate in trade and commodity-
newsletter sources. No peer-reviewed origin located. Do not build on
them. Recorded so the next reader does not mistake them for literature.

Separately: at least one large 3PL states jet fuel has replaced diesel as
the leading indicator of fuel-market stress. Unverified, listed as a
check.

## CONFIDENCE

Q1 (averaging hides load-class variance): high — arithmetic, not
hypothesis.
Q2, Q3, Q4 as causal chains: not stated. Each needs Q1's data to exist
before a gradient means anything.

## FALSIFIERS

- Q1 closes if any series publishes fuel-to-goods-value by load class.
- Q4 closes if national accounts already publish an adjustable versus
  non-adjustable decomposition.
- Q3's non-linearity fails if reefer loads past viability are in practice
  rerouted or downgraded at a rate that smooths the discontinuity.
