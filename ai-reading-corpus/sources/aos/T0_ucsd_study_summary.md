UC San Diego Optical Switching Study
The Study
The work is "Optical beam shaping induced reconfigurable magnetic domains," published in Nature Communications on September 15, 2026 (Nat Commun 17, 10843). First author Muhammad Waleed Khalid and senior author Abdoulaye Ndao led the study, with co-authors including Eric Fullerton (co-director of UC San Diego's Center for Memory and Recording Research) and collaborators at Western Digital and CNRS/Université de Lorraine. Funding came from an AFOSR MURI, the Beckman and Sloan foundations, the Moore Foundation, and NSF's UCSD MRSEC.[ucsd]
The headline result: instead of designing a new magnetic material for all-optical switching (AOS), the team redesigned the light itself. Using specially shaped "circularly polarized vector beams" focused to wavelength-scale spots, they reversed magnetization in Pt/Co multilayer films up to nine bilayers thick — beyond the three-layer ceiling of prior work — without an external magnetic field and without depending on the light's polarization handedness (Nature Communications).
Experimental Setup
•	Material: sputtered Ta(3 nm)/Pt(2 nm)/[Pt(0.7 nm)/Co(0.6 nm)]N/Pt(2.7 nm) films, N = 1–10, with perpendicular magnetic anisotropy (PMA), on glass (Nature Communications)
•	Laser: Spectra-Physics Solstice Ace Ti:sapphire amplifier — 120 fs pulses, λ = 800 nm, 1 kHz repetition rate (Nature Communications)
•	Beam shaping: Gaussian beam → iris → S-waveplate (azimuthal polarization) → vortex phase plate (VPP) of topological charge ℓ = ±1 → focusing objectives of NA 0.20–0.80, giving 1/e² spot diameters of 1.92 down to 0.96 μm; domains imaged by Faraday microscopy and MFM (Nature Communications)
Key Equations
The paper's formalism is almost entirely optical — the magnetic reversal itself is explained physically rather than through a magnetization-dynamics model. Note that no Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert or two-temperature-model equations appear; the equations below are what the authors actually used.
Beam construction (main-text Eqs. 1–2). An azimuthally polarized input field with radial envelope P(r) is decomposed in the circular polarization basis \{\hat{\mathbf{e}}_R, \hat{\mathbf{e}}_L\}:
\mathbf{E}_\phi(r,\phi) = P(r)\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} = \frac{iP(r)}{\sqrt{2}}\left(e^{-i\phi}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_L - e^{+i\phi}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_R\right)
After a vortex phase plate of charge \ell, one circular component loses its on-axis phase singularity and can focus to a bright center:
\mathbf{E}_{VB}(r,\phi) = \frac{iP(r)}{\sqrt{2}}\left[e^{i(\ell-1)\phi}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_L - e^{i(\ell+1)\phi}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_R\right]
The supplement gives the envelope P(r) = E_0 (r/w_0)^{|\ell|} e^{-r^2/w_0^2} and the full component-wise derivation, plus 2D Stokes polarimetry (I, M, C, S) used to verify the focal-plane polarization (supplementary information).
Focal-field calculation (Eq. 3, Richards–Wolf). The focused field of the high-NA objective is computed with the vectorial Debye integral:
E(r_2,\phi_2,z) = -\frac{iC}{\lambda}\int_0^{\theta_{max}}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi} \sin\theta\sqrt{\cos\theta}\, M\, E_t\, e^{ik(z\cos\theta + r_2\sin\theta\cos(\phi-\phi_2))}\, d\phi\, d\theta
where \theta_{max}=\arcsin(\mathrm{NA}), M is a matrix encoding the vectorial polarization transformation during focusing (given explicitly in the supplement), and E_t is the transmitted field (Nature Communications).
Longitudinal-field fraction (Eq. 4). To quantify suppression of the longitudinal component at focus:
I_z = \frac{\int |E_z(x,y)|^2\,dA}{\int \left(|E_x|^2+|E_y|^2+|E_z|^2\right)dA}
This matters because tightly focused circularly polarized Gaussian beams acquire a spurious longitudinal field E_z that grows with NA; the azimuthally symmetric vector beam has E_z(0)=0 by symmetry, keeping polarization clean at focus (Nature Communications).
Dipolar field of the surrounding film (supplement). The demagnetizing (dipolar) field above a uniformly magnetized film of thickness t:
B_z(0^+) \approx \frac{\mu_0 M_s}{2} \quad (\text{large thin film})
Kaplan–Gehring domain-size model (supplement). The equilibrium domain size D that minimizes total energy (domain wall vs. dipolar energy):
D = t\,\exp\left[\frac{\pi D_0}{2t} + \ln(\pi) - 1 + \left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\ln 2\right)\right], \quad D_0 = \frac{\sigma}{E_d}, \quad \mu = 1+\frac{E_d}{K_u}
with E_d = 2\pi M_s^2 the demagnetizing energy density and \sigma = 4\sqrt{A_{ex}K_u} the domain-wall energy. Computed equilibrium domains shrink from ~2.7 mm (N=1) to ~0.9 μm (N=9) — this competition between domain size and optical spot size is the heart of the paper's physics (supplementary information).
Physical Principles of the Switching
The mechanism is a competition between three effects, as the authors interpret it:
1. Photothermal nucleation. The 120 fs pulse deposits energy faster than the lattice can respond; the irradiated region is driven to a low-coercivity state near the Curie temperature, and a reversed magnetic domain nucleates. The first pulses create the reversed nucleus; subsequent pulses grow it. Switching is cumulative and multi-pulse — explicitly not the single-shot "toggle" mechanism of ferrimagnets like GdFeCo, which the paper notes requires ferrimagnetic sublattice dynamics that purely ferromagnetic Pt/Co lacks (Nature Communications).
2. Magnetic circular dichroism (MCD) — the helicity-dependent path. Right- and left-circular light is absorbed slightly differently depending on magnetization direction. In thin stacks (N=1–2), MCD-driven domain-wall motion steers growth, so the final state tracks the light's handedness: classic all-optical helicity-dependent switching (AO-HDS) (Nature Communications).
3. Dipolar stabilization — the helicity-independent path. As N increases, the magnetostatic energy of the surrounding unexcited film grows, and its demagnetizing field (\sim\mu_0 M_s/2) biases the hot region to relax into a single reversed domain. Shrinking the spot below ~1.36 μm concentrates the energy density, accelerates thermal cycling, and shrinks the volume that must be stabilized — so the surrounding film's dipolar field overwhelms the weak MCD bias and both helicities produce identical outcomes (AO-HIS). The measured phase map: N=3 shows the HD→HI transition at ~1.36 μm spot diameter; N=4 is HI at all tested spot sizes; N=5–9 are HI only under wavelength-scale focusing (NA 0.80); N≥10 forms stripe domains (PMA vs. magnetostatics) that block uniform reversal (Nature Communications). MFM measured an average switched width of ~0.76 ± 0.09 μm for N=3 (supplementary information).
A critical control experiment underscores the beam engineering: with comparable Gaussian beams, N=3–5 films suffered permanent thermal damage and N=6–9 formed random multidomain states — the vector beam reaches the switching threshold deterministically where a Gaussian beam cannot (supplementary information).
Current Technical Challenges
Light-source integration (the explicitly stated hurdle). The Ti:sapphire femtosecond amplifier is a benchtop system that cannot yet be readily built into computer chips or conventional storage hardware. The team's stated paths forward: find magnetic materials that respond to lasers more easily incorporated into electronics, and develop optical structures (e.g., photonic confinement) to shrink the beam toward a few hundred nanometers.[optica-opn +1]
Write speed is not yet established. The "more than 1,000× faster than magnetic-field writing" figure is a prospective estimate, not a measurement — a 120 fs pulse does not mean a 120 fs write. Switching requires many pulses: the sweep conditions used correspond to roughly 200–2,000 pulses per illuminated area (e.g., ~200 pulses/μm² at 5 μm/s scan, 1 kHz, 1 μm spot), and the total time to complete one write, the minimum reliable pulse count, and the maximum usable pulse rate have not been determined (XenoSpectrum, supplementary information).[xenospectrum]
Bit density. The demonstrated ~0.96 μm spots and ~0.76 μm switched regions are far above hard-drive bit dimensions, and no bit-size or adjacent-bit spacing was demonstrated. Because the stabilizing dipolar bias comes from the surrounding magnetized film, independent addressing of bits in a dense array remains an open question.[xenospectrum]
Thickness ceiling. For N≥10 bilayers, stripe-domain formation prevents uniform reversal, capping the thermal-stability benefit of thicker media — and prior work had already shown thickness beyond three layers suppressed switching under Gaussian illumination, which this work only overcame to N=9 (Nature Communications, UC San Diego Today).[ucsd]
Unmeasured device metrics. Retention time, rewrite endurance, adjacent-region cross-talk, and whole-system energy consumption (laser and optics included, not just absorbed light) were not evaluated. The authors themselves flag material-dependent switching thresholds and the dynamic evolution of magnetic domains as needed future work (Nature Communications).[xenospectrum]
Operational fragility. The vector beam's polarization purity degrades rapidly with axial defocus (the "polarization filter" effect), demanding precise sample placement; and the initial magnetization is still prepared with a permanent magnet before writing — no external field is needed during the write itself, but field-free initialization was not demonstrated (supplementary information, XenoSpectrum). Khalid also noted the team spent substantial effort replicating the unusual results to convince reviewers the effect was not a fluke.[ucsd +1]
The bottom line: this is a physics-of-mechanism demonstration, not a memory prototype. Its real contribution is showing that beam shape and focal confinement — not material composition — can select the switching mechanism, which the authors frame as a scalable route toward integrating ultrafast spintronic memory with on-chip photonics (Nature Communications).
